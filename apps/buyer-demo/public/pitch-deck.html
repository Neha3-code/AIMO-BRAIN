01 / The problem

Buying food is easy.
Agreeing the trade is not.

A kitchen needs the right product, at the right price, with the right ingredients and delivery. A supplier has its own stock, credit limits and approval rules.

A product match is not the same as an acceptable transaction.
BUYER SIDE“Will this work for my kitchen?”Budget · allergens · recipe · carbon · delivery
SUPPLIER SIDE“Can we accept this order?”Availability · commercial limits · approvals
Two sets of rules One trusted trade
02 / The solution

Two agents. Two independent rulebooks.
One trusted transaction.

AIMO BRAIN lets autonomous purchasing agents work across the buyer–supplier boundary without giving either side control over the other's policies.

Buyer-controlled

AIMO BRAIN buyer agent

Protects the kitchen's requirements.

  • Budget and product preferences
  • Allergens and recipe compatibility
  • Carbon limits and delivery tolerance
  • Buyer-owned approval rules
Supplier-controlled

Aimo supplier agent

Applies the supplier's commercial constraints.

  • Credit limits
  • Commercial approval ceilings
  • Supplier-owned human approvals
  • Availability and quote conditions
Kitchen request→AI understands & proposes→Buyer policy+Supplier policy→Order decision
AI proposes. Independent policy engines decide. Neither party overrides the other's rules.

05 / AI-assisted discovery & planning

The AI proposes the basket.
It also says when not to trust it.

Two ways into the same policy pipeline — find a product you already have in mind, or describe a need and let Nemotron work out what to buy.

Find products

Search 17,546 real SKUs

Natural-language search over the Junction26 catalogue, not just exact names.

  • “Something creamy and lactose-free for a dessert” → ranked, real SKUs
  • Every result ships with a plain-language reason
  • A relevant-looking match that's out of stock or carries a banned allergen is labelled “blocked by buyer policy” — never recommended as if it were clean
Plan an order

From a dish to a basket

Start from a seasonal occasion or describe your own menu and covers.

  • Pikkujoulu, winter game, root vegetables — or your own dish
  • Ingredients → matched products → quantities, sized to covers
  • Dilution-aware math for concentrates; surplus and low-confidence lines flagged before you evaluate

07 / Why it matters

Automation without
giving up control.

The value is different for each side — and shared where trust matters most.

01 / THE KITCHEN

Less manual coordination

Buyer requirements travel with the request. Alternatives — and whole baskets — are checked against the kitchen's own rules before an order proceeds.

02 / THE SUPPLIER

Commercial authority stays put

Supplier-side limits and approvals remain supplier-owned. A buyer agent cannot waive them.

03 / BOTH SIDES

Decisions you can explain

Exceptions are surfaced, approval ownership is clear and decision reasons are recorded for review.

Exception-ready

When reality changes

Out of stock. Partial availability. A substitute that breaks a recipe. A delivery date outside tolerance. The workflow handles the exception instead of hiding it.

Evidence-led

Every decision has context

Who made the decision, which policy applied, what evidence was available and why the workflow continued, stopped or asked for approval.

Autonomy without surrendering authority.
08 / What's next

The hard parts are solved.
Here's what we'd harden next.

This is a Junction26 prototype on sandbox data. Three concrete steps on the way to production.

01 / COMMERCE

Real supplier authentication

Move from the hackathon's API-key stand-in to the documented OAuth2 client-credentials flow, so any authorized buyer agent can trade, not just this demo.

02 / DATA

Structured pack data

Replace name-parsed pack sizes and dilution ratios with structured catalogue fields, so every order quantity is calculated, never estimated from text.

03 / SCALE

More suppliers, one basket

Extend the planner to compare and split a basket across multiple suppliers, under the same independent buyer/supplier policy model.

A prototype that's honest about what's next is more trustworthy than one that pretends to be finished.
